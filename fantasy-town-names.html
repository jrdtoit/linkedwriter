How to use it

Pick the terrain your settlement sits in — coast, mountains, forest, desert, a river crossing, or somewhere darker — and press the seal. Each style draws from its own vocabulary, so a desert well stop will never accidentally sound like a fishing port. If you are filling a whole map, work region by region: draw a batch for one valley, keep the best three, and let their shared sound set the accent for everything nearby.

Tips for naming settlements

  • Name the reason before the place. Every lasting town exists because of something — a ford, a mine, a shrine, a well. A name that carries its reason (Ferryford, Goldbasin) never needs an explanation at the table.
  • Let the important places have the longer names. Capitals and quest hubs can afford three syllables and a story; a village the party passes once should be one plain compound and no homework.
  • Steal your own roots. If one town on the river is Twinford, the next one downstream wants to be Twinfalls or Lowferry — maps feel real when names rhyme with each other.

Naming a settlement by size: village, town, or city

A village is named after the one thing it has. A dell, a mill, a stand of ash trees. Village names are short, plain, and slightly worn — Ashdell, Mossham, Sandwell — because the people who named the place were describing it to neighbours, not advertising it to strangers. If a name sounds like it would look good on a banner, it is too grand for a village.

A town is named after what it does for the region around it: the ford it guards, the market it hosts, the bridge it tolls. Town names are functional and a little proud — Bridgemarket, Kingsmarket, Ferryford — and they tend to spread, because everyone in the region uses the name as a direction. This is also where trade-crossing names earn their keep: a town at the meeting of two roads and a river will be named for the meeting, not the scenery.

A city can carry freight a village cannot: a founder, a saint, a victory, a boast. City names are where two-part constructions and titles belong, and where a name from the realm and map generator — a kingdom's name worn by its capital — fits naturally. The fastest way to make a map feel lived-in is simply to respect the ladder: villages plain, towns useful, cities grand. Players feel the difference without ever being told, and the inn they stay in — named with the tavern name generator — lands in a place that already makes sense.

One root, a whole region

The cheapest worldbuilding trick on any map is repetition with variation. Real regions name their settlements out of one shared vocabulary, worn smooth by the same mouths for centuries — which is why one river can hold a Twinford, a Twinfalls and a Lowferry without any of them sounding like a coincidence. When you settle a new valley, pick two or three roots and reuse them shamelessly: a prefix for the ruling family's old estate, a suffix for the terrain every village shares. The tavern, the dungeon underneath the hill, and the three hamlets around it should all sound like they were named by cousins. That family resemblance is what makes a player trust a map — the names feel discovered rather than assigned, and a discovered world is one they will cross a desert to reach the far side of.

Frequently asked questions

How do I name a fantasy town?

Start from the land, not the dictionary. Ask what the settlement is for (a port, a crossing, a mine, a shrine) and what it sits on (a river, a pass, a well), then weld those two facts together: Ferryford, Goldbasin, Stonehaven. A town named after its reason to exist sounds settled the moment it lands on the map.

Should a village, a town, and a city be named differently?

Yes — size has a sound. Villages take small, plain compounds named after one local feature (Ashdell, Mossham). Towns take functional names — the ford, the market, the mill. Cities can carry grander freight: a founder, a saint, a boast, or a two-part name with a title. If every dot on your map sounds like a capital, none of them do.

Should nearby towns share parts of their names?

Often, yes. Settlements in one valley grow from the same root language, so a shared element — three towns ending in -ford along one river, or a family of Briar- names in one forest — makes a region feel related. Keep the shared element boring and vary the other half; real maps are full of near-repeats.

Is this page different from the realm and map name generator?

Yes. The realm generator names countries, wilds, and regions — the big shapes on the map. This page names the settlements inside them: the towns and villages your party actually sleeps in. Most maps need both, in that order.

Can I use these names in a published novel, game, or adventure?

Yes, generated and listed town names are free to use in any personal or commercial project without attribution.
