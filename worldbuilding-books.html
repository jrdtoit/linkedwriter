Worldbuilding Books & Tools for DMs: Build a Realm You Can Actually Run

Most worldbuilding advice tells you to buy a shelf. This guide works the other way round: start with what you can make free, then buy only the book or tool that solves the problem you are stuck on — a realm with no politics, a region with nothing in it, a map you cannot draw, or notes that no longer agree with each other.

How this guide was put together

Honestly, and briefly: this is not a hands-on testing roundup. Every book below was chosen from its publisher's own description of what it covers, cross-checked against how the tabletop and writing communities actually use and recommend it. The organising principle is the job to be done, not a star rating — the right book for a DM whose realm feels flat is a different book from the right one for a DM whose map is a blank page.

Start free: name and map the realm before you buy anything

A playable realm needs five things: a name, a rough map, a handful of settlements, one live conflict, and somewhere dangerous. None requires a purchase. Name the realm and its wilds with the realm and map name generator, settle its towns with the fantasy town name generator, give its peoples names and flavour with the race and clan name generator, and put something worth fearing under the hill with the dungeon name generator and a printable layout from the dungeon map generator. Sketch the map on paper, however badly. Write the conflict in two sentences: who wants what, and who is in their way.

Do that first. A campaign that starts this Friday beats a world bible that is perfect next year — and building the free version tells you exactly where your wall is. If the realm still feels like a list of names, your problem is structure: politics, faith, history. If it feels real but empty, your problem is stocking: towns, hooks, dungeons. If it all exists only inside your head, your problem is the map or the notebook. Buy for the wall you actually hit.

Building the realm: politics, faiths, and conflicts

The reference point in this category is the Kobold Guide to Worldbuilding, published by Kobold Press and edited by Janna Silverstein. It is a collection of essays by working game designers — among them Keith Baker, Wolfgang Baur, Monte Cook, David "Zeb" Cook, Jeff Grubb, Chris Pramas and Michael A. Stackpole, with an introduction by Ken Scholes — on how invented worlds are actually constructed: what to build first, what to leave deliberately unfinished, and how a world's history, geography and beliefs push back on the people living in it. Because it is essays rather than tables, it is the book to reach for when your realm has places but no pressure — no rival faiths, no old grudges, no reason for the borders to be where they are. A second volume, edited by John Joseph Adams, extends the same approach with newer contributors.

For writers, and for DMs who think like writers, the companion recommendation is On Writing and Worldbuilding by Timothy Hickson — the craft underneath the setting: how empires rise and fall, how power, communication and commerce shape a world, and how to deliver it without an exposition lecture. The Kobold Guide is the game master's book; Hickson's is the storyteller's. Neither will stock your world for you, though. That is the next section.

Stocking the region: towns, taverns, dungeons, and hooks

When the realm exists but the map is empty — three named towns and blank parchment between them — the tool the community reaches for is Matt Finch's Tome of Adventure Design, first published by Frog God Games and now available in a revised edition from Mythmere Games. By its publisher's description it is a comprehensive, start-to-finish resource for designing fantasy adventures, and most of its bulk is random-generation tables: starting points, villainous motives, locations, dungeon design, and monster design. It will not tell you what your world means. It gives you something specific and playable every time you roll — the correct cure for a beautiful, empty setting.

Its companion volume is the Tome of Worldbuilding (also styled Tome of World Building), by Matt Finch from Mythmere Games — a system-neutral guidebook with hundreds of generation tables covering continents and cataclysms, cultures and countries, cities, deities, personalities and legendary locations. Use Adventure Design when the problem is "nothing is happening here this week" and Worldbuilding when it is "this place would not exist in a real world." Pair either with the fast tools here: roll a settlement, name it with the town name generator, name its inn with the tavern name generator, and name what is buried underneath it with the dungeon name generator. Tables give you the bones; names give them a local accent.

The map on the table: map-making books and tools

If your world exists in notes but not on paper, you have three honest routes. The first is to learn to draw it. How to Draw Fantasy Art and RPG Maps by Jared Blando (IMPACT Books) teaches fantasy cartography step by step — coastlines, mountains, forests, icons for towns and fortresses, lettering, and heraldry — with more than thirty demonstrations that build a complete world map, by hand and digitally. Blando is a professional cartographer who has drawn maps for major RPG publishers, and the book's virtue for a DM is that a hand-drawn map can look deliberately old in a way players forgive instantly.

The second route is software. Inkarnate is a browser-based map maker with a free tier, widely used for world and regional maps because there is nothing to install. Wonderdraft is a desktop program bought once rather than subscribed to, aimed at the same scales, and liked by people who expect to keep redrawing the same continent for years. Neither has any connection to this site — the choice between them is mostly browser versus owning a program.

The third route is the one this site is built on: do not draw the whole world at all. Generate the dungeon when you need one with the dungeon map generator, keep the world map as a rough sketch with good names on it, and spend your drawing time on the region the party is actually in. Most campaigns are played on about ten percent of the map their DM imagined.

Daily life in the world: medieval society references

The detail that makes a fantasy town feel lived-in is rarely magical. It is economic: what the miller charges, who judges a theft, what people eat in winter, why the village owes the lord labour in harvest week. Two references cover that ground better than any game book. Life in a Medieval Village by Joseph and Frances Gies (Harper Perennial) reconstructs one English village around the year 1300 — its fields, families, church, court and working year — and its companion volumes do the same for the city and the castle. One chapter will give you more believable tavern talk and village grievances than a shelf of setting books.

For the same material in game terms, there is A Magical Medieval Society: Western Europe by Joseph Browning and Suzi Yee (Expeditious Retreat Press), now in a third edition — the historical structure of kingdoms, manors, cities and economics, with generation systems a GM can use directly, and one useful question throughout: what changes when real magic exists inside it? Read the Gies books to understand the world; read Browning and Yee to run it. Then name the result with the fantasy town name generator.

Campaign journals and setting bibles

Every long campaign eventually fails the same way: not at the table, but in the notes. The NPC the party loved in March had a different name in June; the treaty that started the war was never written down. A campaign journal or setting bible — one fixed place where names, factions, timelines and session notes live — is the least glamorous purchase in this guide and the one most likely to still be in use in a year. A structured journal suits a DM who wants prompts; a plain, sturdy notebook suits one who just needs it to survive a year of being carried to games. Digital notes work too, provided there is one document rather than forty.

Build it from your own material first: copy in the names your world actually uses — the realm from the realm generator, the clans from the race and clan generator, the towns and taverns the party has visited — and let the bible grow around the campaign as played, not as planned. A bible describing the world the players have met is a tool. One describing a world they never reached is a novel draft.

How to choose: by the job you are stuck on

  • The realm feels flat — no politics, faith or conflict: Kobold Guide to Worldbuilding (essays on how worlds hold together), or On Writing and Worldbuilding if you think like a writer.
  • The region is empty — nothing between the towns: Tome of Adventure Design for adventures, locations and hooks on demand.
  • The world itself does not feel real: Tome of Worldbuilding for continents, cultures, religions and settlements, generated table by table.
  • There is no map, or the map embarrasses you: Blando's How to Draw Fantasy Art and RPG Maps to learn by hand; Inkarnate or Wonderdraft if you would rather revise on screen.
  • Towns feel like cardboard — no daily life: Life in a Medieval Village for the history, A Magical Medieval Society: Western Europe for the same material in game terms.
  • You keep contradicting your own notes: a campaign journal or setting bible — any format you will actually carry and open.
  • Nothing is stuck, you are just browsing: buy nothing. Use the generators, run the next session, and let a real problem choose the book.

What to skip, and what to borrow first

Skip the complete shelf. Worldbuilding books are unusually borrowable — most are backlist titles libraries hold, and the essay collections can be read once, mined for the three ideas your realm needs, and returned. Borrow the Kobold Guide and Hickson first; buy the one you keep reaching back for. Skip any tool bought to avoid starting, too: a second map program will not fix a world with no conflict, and a premium journal will not write your session notes. Use the books above the way the generators here are used — at the moment of need, for the region the party enters next.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best worldbuilding book for a DM?

It depends on the job you are stuck on. For the big questions — politics, faith, conflict, and how a realm holds together — the Kobold Guide to Worldbuilding (Kobold Press, edited by Janna Silverstein) is the most recommended starting point. For filling a region with adventures, towns and hooks, Matt Finch's Tome of Adventure Design and Tome of Worldbuilding are table-driven tools rather than essays. Pick by problem, not by reputation.

Do I need to buy worldbuilding books to run a homebrew campaign?

No. A playable realm needs a name, a map you can read, a handful of settlements, one conflict, and somewhere dangerous to go — all of which you can make free with the generators on this site and a notebook. Books earn their place when you keep hitting the same wall: realms that feel flat, regions with nothing in them, or notes you can no longer keep consistent.

What is the difference between the Tome of Adventure Design and the Tome of Worldbuilding?

Both are by Matt Finch and built largely from random-generation tables. The Tome of Adventure Design is about adventures: locations, missions, villains, dungeons and monsters. The Tome of Worldbuilding is its companion volume about the world around those adventures: continents, cultures, countries, cities, religions and personalities. If your problem is "nothing is happening here", start with Adventure Design; if it is "this place does not feel real", start with Worldbuilding.

Should I draw my fantasy map by hand or use software?

Either works. Hand-drawing, taught step by step in Jared Blando's How to Draw Fantasy Art and RPG Maps, gives you a map with a personal hand and no subscription. Software such as Inkarnate (browser-based) or Wonderdraft (a desktop program) is faster to revise when your world changes mid-campaign. Choose based on how often you expect to redraw the map, not on which output looks more impressive.

Can I use these books and tools for a novel instead of a game?

Yes. Most of the books in this guide are system-neutral or written for writers as much as for game masters. On Writing and Worldbuilding by Timothy Hickson is aimed squarely at fiction writers, and the medieval life references work for anyone who needs a believable village, town or castle. The game-specific table books are still useful to novelists as idea generators.
